We asked the same 24 test questions to all three approaches: 8 that reuse a policy's wording, 8 paraphrased ones, 3 where a related policy exists but lacks the requested detail, and 5 that no policy covers. Every answer was checked against the full policy database by a separate LLM judge (gemini-3.1-flash-lite). Rules-based keyword search answered almost instantly (about 3 ms) and used no tokens. It found the right policy for all 8 questions that reuse the policy's wording, but for only 2 of the 8 paraphrased ones. It also said "no policy" for only 2 of the 5 questions nothing covers. Because it always returns the closest keyword match, half of its answers were unsupported by the database; for example, it answered a bereavement question with the Meeting Etiquette Policy. Both Gemini approaches (gemini-3.5-flash-lite) cited the correct policy for every direct and paraphrased question and correctly reported that no policy applied in all 5 uncovered cases. Their few unsupported answers (3 of 24 without an index, 2 of 24 with one) all came from questions where the policy lacks the requested detail. In those cases the model restated or overstated the policy instead of saying the detail is missing, for example by saying unused vacation days roll over "without a specified limit". Response times were almost identical (median 1.78 s without an index and 1.81 s with one), because the embedding lookup takes about as long as the shorter prompt saves. The vector index did cut token use from about 2,300 to about 210 per question.

The preferred approach is the LLM with a vector index. It matches the LLM without an index on paraphrased questions and on declining to answer when no policy applies. It had the lowest rate of unsupported answers and uses roughly 11 times fewer tokens. The token difference matters more than the speed difference. Putting the whole database in every prompt only works here because the 98 policies are one sentence each. A real policy handbook would quickly make every question slow and expensive, while the vector approach sends only the three most relevant policies no matter how large the database grows. Rules-based search is still useful as a free, instant fallback when the LLM service is unavailable. It should not be used on its own, though, because it cannot tell when it has no answer. These results come from a small test set scored by another LLM, so they show tendencies rather than exact rates. The main remaining risk is overstating a policy when a detail is missing, and a stricter instruction to flag missing details could reduce it.
